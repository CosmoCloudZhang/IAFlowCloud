\section{Cosmological background and intrinsic alignment}
\label{sec:background}

The motivation for modelling intrinsic alignment follows from the use of
galaxy shapes to study cosmic expansion and the growth of structure. This
section introduces the cosmological model and its observational tests,
then develops the connection between weak gravitational lensing, intrinsic
alignment, and the response-reconstruction problem investigated here.

\subsection{Cosmic expansion and the standard cosmological model}
\label{sec:cosmology-background}

Cosmology studies the Universe as a whole: its contents, history, and
large-scale structure. The hot Big Bang model describes an early Universe
that was much hotter and denser than it is today. As space expanded, the
Universe cooled. The Big Bang was not an explosion from one place into
empty space; it describes the expansion of space itself. A key piece of
evidence is the \emph{cosmic microwave background} (CMB), light that began
travelling freely when the Universe was about 380,000 years old. The Planck
satellite mapped its small temperature variations, which carry information
about conditions in the early Universe \citep{2020A&A...641A...1P}.
Figure~\ref{fig:background-planck} shows these variations. Their statistical
properties help constrain the cosmological model used to describe the
Universe's contents and evolution.

   rather than a map of present-day dark matter. Image credit:
   ESA/Planck Collaboration \citep{2020A&A...641A...1P}.}
  {fig:background-planck}

Expansion also stretches travelling light to longer wavelengths. We describe
this \emph{cosmological redshift} by
\begin{equation}
  1+z=\frac{\lambda_{\mathrm{observed}}}{\lambda_{\mathrm{emitted}}}
      =\frac{1}{a},
  \label{eq:cosmological-redshift}
\end{equation}
where $a$ is the scale factor, which tracks cosmic expansion and equals
one today. Thus $z=1$ means that the wavelength has doubled since emission.
Larger cosmological redshifts generally let us study earlier times. They
are not distances by themselves: converting redshift to distance requires
an expansion model.

The standard cosmological model, called $\Lambda$CDM, includes a cosmological
constant ($\Lambda$) and cold dark matter (CDM). It is also called the
concordance model because it provides a common description of evidence
from the CMB, galaxy distributions, and other observations. In this model,
about $5\%$ of today's total energy density is ordinary matter, $27\%$ is dark
matter, and $68\%$ is dark energy; these are approximate, model-dependent
fractions \citep{2020A&A...641A...6P}. Ordinary matter includes atoms in stars
and gas. Dark matter is inferred through gravity, and its particle identity
remains unknown. ``Cold'' means that it moved slowly enough in the early
Universe to allow small structures to grow. The symbol $\Lambda$ represents
a cosmological constant, whose energy density remains constant as space
expands and can drive accelerated expansion. Whether dark energy is exactly
this constant, and why it exists, remain open questions.

\subsection{Observational constraints on dark energy}
\label{sec:dark-energy-background}

To test the expansion history, astronomers need ways to compare distances
at different times. The Dark Energy Spectroscopic Instrument (DESI)
\citep{2025PhRvD.112h3515A} measures galaxy and quasar redshifts over a large
volume. Its measurements reveal \emph{baryon acoustic oscillations} (BAO): a
preferred separation in the distribution of matter, left by sound waves in
the early Universe. This provides a statistical ruler whose apparent size
changes with distance.

The second DESI data release, DR2, gives precise BAO measurements.
Two quantities used to express these constraints are $\Omega_{\rm m}$,
the present matter density divided by the critical density for a spatially
flat Universe, and $H_0r_d$. Here $H_0$ is today's expansion rate and $r_d$
is the comoving length of the BAO ruler, with the overall expansion factored
out. BAO constrains $H_0r_d$ more directly than $H_0$ alone.
Figure~\ref{fig:background-desi} shows the allowed combinations and a test
of changing dark energy.

Dark energy's \emph{equation-of-state parameter}, $w$, is its pressure
divided by its energy density. A cosmological constant has $w=-1$. A common
test allows
\begin{equation}
  w(a)=w_0+w_a(1-a),
  \label{eq:dark-energy-evolution}
\end{equation}
where $w_0$ is the value today and $w_a$ controls how it changes. The constant
case is $(w_0,w_a)=(-1,0)$. Type Ia supernovae provide another distance
indicator: after correcting for differences between them, their brightnesses
can be compared. In the DR2 BAO analysis, combining DESI with CMB
measurements and different supernova samples gives a preference for evolution
at about $2.8$--$4.2\sigma$, depending on the combination
\citep{2025PhRvD.112h3515A}. Here $\sigma$ expresses statistical significance
on a standard-deviation scale. This indication depends on the data and model
assumptions, so it is not a confirmed discovery. A later DESI analysis
\citep{2026arXiv260727410D} adds information from absorption by intergalactic
hydrogen. Its joint constraints also retain a preference for evolving dark
energy, while showing that the strength of the evidence depends on the
observations and modelling assumptions.

Confirmed evolution would challenge the cosmological-constant explanation
and have major implications for fundamental physics. Weak gravitational
lensing offers complementary evidence through the growth and distribution
of matter, with different sources of measurement error. Such a check must
account for shared data and assumptions; a combined analysis is not
automatically independent.

\subsection{Weak gravitational lensing and matter clustering}
\label{sec:lensing-background}

Gravity changes the paths of light rays. A large mass can produce obvious
arcs or multiple images of a background galaxy, called strong gravitational
lensing. In weak gravitational lensing, the changes are much smaller.
\emph{Convergence}, written $\kappa$, describes isotropic focusing: a small
circular image changes size but stays circular. \emph{Shear}, written
$\gamma$, describes stretching in one direction and compression in the
perpendicular direction. A circle then becomes an ellipse, as illustrated
in Figure~\ref{fig:background-lensing} \citep{2001PhR...340..291B}.

\backgroundfigure{background_lensing_density.pdf}
  {Light-path geometry; convergence and shear; spatial wavelength.}
  {Weak gravitational lensing geometry, image distortions, and spatial scale.
   Intervening matter deflects light from distant galaxies. Convergence changes
   apparent size, while shear changes shape; distortions are exaggerated for
   clarity. Density waves over equal distance intervals illustrate the
   relation between spatial wavelength and wavenumber. These are schematic
   examples, not measured density fluctuations \citep{2001PhR...340..291B}.
   Diagram prepared with AI assistance; source details are in the figure guide.}
  {fig:background-lensing}

The matter distribution is inhomogeneous, with clusters, filaments, and
underdense regions. We describe this \emph{density field} by its
density contrast,
\begin{equation}
  \delta(\boldsymbol{x},z)=
  \frac{\rho(\boldsymbol{x},z)-\bar\rho(z)}{\bar\rho(z)},
  \label{eq:matter-density-contrast}
\end{equation}
where $\rho$ is the matter density at position $\boldsymbol{x}$ and
$\bar\rho$ is its mean at that time. Positive $\delta$ denotes an overdense
region. Instead of tracking every structure, we can describe the typical
strength of fluctuations on different scales with the \emph{matter power
spectrum}, $P_\delta(k,z)$. The wavenumber
\begin{equation}
  k=\frac{2\pi}{\lambda_{\mathrm{spatial}}}
  \label{eq:spatial-wavenumber}
\end{equation}
labels a spatial wavelength: small $k$ means large structures, and large
$k$ means small structures. This wavelength describes a density fluctuation,
not the light used in Equation~\eqref{eq:cosmological-redshift}.
We use comoving lengths, which factor out the overall expansion. A
megaparsec (Mpc) is about 3.26 million light-years, so $k$ can be expressed
in $\mathrm{Mpc}^{-1}$. The power spectrum summarizes how densities at pairs
of positions are related, called two-point statistics; it does not specify
the full arrangement of matter \citep{2001PhR...340..291B}.

Astronomers estimate galaxy shapes, correct for image blurring and measurement
bias, and estimate galaxy redshifts. They then measure how pairs of shapes
are correlated at different angular separations and in different redshift
groups. This statistical weak gravitational lensing signal is called
\emph{cosmic shear}. Its prediction combines the matter power spectrum with
the geometry between the observer, intervening matter, and source galaxies.
Matter over a broad range of distances contributes to each measurement.
Weak gravitational lensing therefore tests both the growth of structure and
the expansion history, but does not directly give $P_\delta$ at a single
scale and redshift.

\subsection{Cosmic-shear surveys and the \texorpdfstring{$S_8$}{S8} tension}
\label{sec:surveys-background}

A useful quantity for comparing cosmic-shear measurements of matter
clustering is
\begin{equation}
  S_8=\sigma_8\sqrt{\Omega_{\rm m}/0.3}.
  \label{eq:clustering-amplitude}
\end{equation}
Here $\sigma_8$ measures the root-mean-square density contrast predicted
today by linear growth theory, after averaging within spheres of radius
$8h^{-1}\,\mathrm{Mpc}$, with
$h=H_0/(100\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}\,Mpc^{-1}})$. Larger $\sigma_8$ means stronger
matter fluctuations. Cosmic shear constrains the combination $S_8$
particularly well.

Three major imaging surveys are the Kilo-Degree Survey (KiDS)
\citep{2025A&A...703A.158W}, the Dark Energy Survey (DES)
\citep{2022PhRvD.105b3514A}, and the Hyper Suprime-Cam survey (HSC)
\citep{2023PhRvD.108l3518L}. Often called Stage III surveys, they use
different combinations of area, depth, and image quality to constrain this
quantity.

  {Published Stage III cosmic-shear constraints compared with Planck.}
  {Published cosmic-shear constraints in the $\Omega_{\rm m}$--$S_8$ plane,
   compared with Planck. The legend identifies each survey and analysis
   statistic. These are different analyses, rather than fits with identical
   modelling choices. The small inset indicates the smoothing kernel used
   to display the distributions; it is not another measurement. Adapted from
   the left panel of Figure 14 in \citet{2025A&A...703A.158W}, under CC BY 4.0.}

Some earlier shear analyses favoured a lower $S_8$ than the value predicted
from the CMB within $\Lambda$CDM. This difference is known as the
\emph{$S_8$ tension}. The KiDS-1000 analysis \citep{2021A&A...645A.104A}
found a difference of about $3\sigma$; its later reanalysis
\citep{2023A&A...679A.133L} reduced this to about $2.3\sigma$.
KiDS-Legacy \citep{2025A&A...703A.158W} is now consistent with Planck, with
$S_8=0.815^{+0.016}_{-0.021}$. The shift mainly reflects improved redshift
calibration, together with new survey area and improved image reduction.

The degree of agreement with Planck varies across surveys and analysis
choices. DES Year 6 \citep{2026arXiv260210065D} reports
$S_8=0.798^{+0.014}_{-0.015}$ or $0.783^{+0.019}_{-0.015}$ for two different
alignment models, discussed in Section~\ref{sec:ia-models-background}.
These are alternative analyses of the same observations, not two independent
measurements. HSC Year 3 \citep{2023PhRvD.108l3518L} finds
$S_8=0.769^{+0.031}_{-0.034}$ from shape correlation functions. Differences
of roughly $2\sigma$ remain in some comparisons. Figure~\ref{fig:background-surveys}
shows a published comparison of the three Stage III surveys with Planck.
The figure predates the Year 6 results discussed here; it is a comparison
across surveys, not a history of every data release. These results indicate
reduced tension in some analyses, while residual differences remain in others.

The next generation, often called Stage IV, includes Euclid
\citep{2025A&A...697A...1E}, the Vera C. Rubin Observatory's Legacy Survey
of Space and Time \citep{2019ApJ...873..111I}, and the Nancy Grace Roman
Space Telescope \citep{2021MNRAS.507.1746E}. Their programmes combine wide
sky coverage, repeated imaging, and sharp space-based images in different
ways. Smaller statistical errors make systematic errors more important.
These include shape and redshift calibration, the effect of gas and stars
on the matter distribution, and intrinsic alignment. Collecting more galaxies
alone does not remove these uncertainties.

\subsection{Intrinsic alignment and galaxy-shape correlations}
\label{sec:ia-background}

Galaxies form and evolve within gravitational environments that can
influence their shapes and orientations. The gravitational force varies
across a galaxy and its surroundings; these spatial differences constitute
a \emph{tidal field}. Tidal forces can also exert torques that affect
rotation. These mechanisms help explain why intrinsic alignments depend on
galaxy population, environment, scale, and time. Observations and simulations
support parts of this picture, but no single model describes every
population and scale equally well \citep{2025A&ARv..33....5C}.

An image's \emph{ellipticity} records its elongation and orientation. For an
approximately elliptical image with semimajor axis $a_{\rm img}$,
semiminor axis $b_{\rm img}$, and position angle $\varphi$ measured from a
fixed sky axis, one common convention is
\begin{equation}
  |e|=\frac{a_{\rm img}-b_{\rm img}}{a_{\rm img}+b_{\rm img}},
  \qquad
  e_1=|e|\cos(2\varphi),
  \qquad
  e_2=|e|\sin(2\varphi).
  \label{eq:image-ellipticity}
\end{equation}
A round image has $|e|=0$, while a more elongated image has larger $|e|$.
The factor of two accounts for the fact that rotating an ellipse by
$180^\circ$ leaves its shape unchanged. The two components describe both
elongation and orientation. Other ellipticity conventions exist, so a shape
estimator must be calibrated consistently with the definition it uses
\citep{2001PhR...340..291B}.

A telescope records a pixelated image blurred by the instrument and, for
ground-based observations, the atmosphere. Even after correcting this
blurring and measurement bias, an individual galaxy shape is a noisy estimate
of shear because its intrinsic ellipticity is usually larger than the
lensing distortion. To distinguish the contributions, we write the
schematic weak-distortion relation
\begin{equation}
 \underbrace{e^{\mathrm{O}}}_{\text{observed shape}}
 \simeq
 \underbrace{e^{\mathrm{I}}}_{\text{intrinsic shape}}
 +\underbrace{\gamma}_{\text{gravitational shear}}
 +\underbrace{n_e}_{\text{measurement noise}}.
 \label{eq:observed-ellipticity}
\end{equation}
Here O denotes observed and I denotes intrinsic; $n_e$ represents the
measurement error in ellipticity. Each term has two components. This
schematic expression assumes that the estimator's response has been
accounted for; it is not the exact transformation law of the geometrical
ellipticity in Equation~\eqref{eq:image-ellipticity}. The more precise
lensing quantity is the reduced shear,
\begin{equation}
  g=\frac{\gamma}{1-\kappa},
  \label{eq:reduced-shear}
\end{equation}
which approaches $\gamma$ when convergence is small
\citep{2001PhR...340..291B}.

Correlating pairs of shapes produces several contributions. For distinct
galaxies, measurement errors with zero mean that are uncorrelated with one
another and with the signal do not contribute to the expected pair signal.
Expanding the intrinsic-shape and shear contributions gives,
with angular and component labels suppressed,
\begin{equation}
  \big\langle e_i^{\mathrm{O}}e_j^{\mathrm{O}}\big\rangle_{\mathrm{signal}}
  \simeq \mathrm{GG}_{ij}+\mathrm{GI}_{ij}
       +\mathrm{IG}_{ij}+\mathrm{II}_{ij}.
  \label{eq:shape-correlation-components}
\end{equation}
The brackets mean an average over pairs, and $i,j$ label redshift groups.
The contributions have different physical origins:
\begin{itemize}
  \item $\mathrm{GG}$ correlates two gravitational shears. Light from the
  two source galaxies passes through correlated foreground structure,
  producing the desired cosmic-shear contribution.
  \item $\mathrm{II}$ correlates two intrinsic orientations. Galaxies in
  the same or correlated tidal environments can have related orientations
  before lensing. This contribution is usually most relevant for physically
  close pairs.
  \item $\mathrm{GI}$ and $\mathrm{IG}$ correlate an intrinsic orientation
  with a gravitational shear. A foreground galaxy can align with matter
  that also lenses a more distant source; the sources need not be neighbours.
  The ordering identifies which redshift group supplies each contribution.
\end{itemize}
The two cross terms are often grouped as $\mathrm{GI}$, giving the shorthand
$\mathrm{GG}+\mathrm{GI}+\mathrm{II}$. Observations constrain their sum;
their different angular and redshift dependences help a model distinguish
them. Random intrinsic shapes and uncorrelated measurement errors add
variance, often called shape noise, whereas correlated measurement errors
can bias the signal. Figure~\ref{fig:ia-clue} illustrates the physical
connections \citep{2025A&ARv..33....5C}.

\subsection{Intrinsic-alignment models and dimensionality reduction}
\label{sec:ia-models-background}

The \emph{linear alignment} (LA) picture treats intrinsic shape as a
leading-order response to the large-scale tidal field. The widely used
\emph{nonlinear alignment} (NLA) prescription retains this response
structure but uses a nonlinear matter power spectrum to predict the
correlations. Typical survey implementations describe the amplitude and
redshift evolution with a few nuisance parameters \citep{2007NJPh....9..444B}.
NLA is economical, but its name does not make it a complete theory of
nonlinear galaxy formation or guarantee accuracy on every scale.

The \emph{tidal alignment and tidal torquing} (TATT) model adds further
responses, including terms quadratic in the tidal field and effects
associated with how galaxies sample the density field
\citep{2019PhRvD.100j3506B}. It can describe more possibilities, usually
with more adjustable parameters. Model suitability depends on the galaxy
population, physical scales, statistical precision, and the assumed ranges
and probabilities of the parameters, called \emph{priors}.

\backgroundfigure{background_model_tradeoff.pdf}
  {Model flexibility, parameter constraints, and the reconstruction experiment.}
  {Model flexibility and compact response representations. Restricted
   models offer fewer parameters to constrain but allow fewer responses;
   flexible models can describe more responses while introducing poorly
   constrained directions. NLA and TATT provide the physical context
   \citep{2007NJPh....9..444B,2019PhRvD.100j3506B}. Our experiment tests
   reconstruction of a chosen response family. The separate final box lists
   further requirements for cosmological inference.
   Diagram prepared with AI assistance; source details are in the figure guide.}
  {fig:background-model-tradeoff}

Model choice involves a balance between flexibility and the ability to
constrain the parameters. An overly restricted model can attribute part of
the alignment signal to a change in cosmology. A more flexible model can be
harder to constrain and more costly to explore. For example, changing
alignment strength can imitate part of a change in matter clustering.
Such overlapping effects are called \emph{parameter degeneracies}.
Uncertainty in galaxy redshifts can introduce further overlap.
The DES Year 6 comparison \citep{2026arXiv260210065D} illustrates that the
alignment prescription can affect the inferred $S_8$.

Two distinct operations affect these constraints. The physical
\emph{line-of-sight projection} combines contributions from different
distances, which can make distinct scale and redshift dependences look
similar. In the statistical analysis, \emph{marginalization} averages over
uncertain model parameters to obtain constraints on cosmology. Their priors
can affect those constraints. Changing the coordinates used to describe a
model does not by itself resolve these effects.

The reconstruction experiment focuses on the representation of model
responses, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:background-model-tradeoff}. We build
a flexible positive response $\mathcal A_\Theta(k,z)$ within an NLA-inspired
prescription and ask whether PCA or an autoencoder can describe its
variations compactly. The original 13 parameters define the family; the
shorter representation approximates its surfaces. In the reported pilot,
we compare representations with two latent coordinates on the same
validation samples. This response is not the matter power spectrum itself,
nor does it reproduce all the physical terms of TATT. Any eventual use in
cosmology would still need tests of errors in lensing predictions and a
justified way to assign probabilities to the latent coordinates.
Section~\ref{sec:model} defines the response family and the dataset used
to evaluate its reconstruction.
