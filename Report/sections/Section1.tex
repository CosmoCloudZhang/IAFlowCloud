\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Weak gravitational lensing probes the distribution of matter through small
distortions in the observed shapes of distant galaxies. As light travels
towards us, the gravity of intervening matter changes its path. The resulting
distortions are difficult to distinguish from an individual galaxy's own
shape, but correlations measured across many galaxies reveal how matter is
distributed. This provides a way to study dark matter and test how cosmic
structure grows as the Universe expands \citep{2001PhR...340..291B}.

Galaxy shapes also carry information about the environments in which
galaxies form and evolve. Gravitational interactions can produce correlated
intrinsic orientations, even before light is deflected on its journey to
the observer. These \emph{intrinsic alignments} contribute to the shape
correlations used to measure lensing. Modelling their contribution is
therefore necessary to infer the matter distribution and cosmological
parameters reliably \citep{2025A&ARv..33....5C}.

An intrinsic-alignment model must allow for uncertainty in how alignment
varies with spatial scale and cosmic time. Additional adjustable parameters
can describe a wider range of responses, but may also be difficult to
constrain because different parameter combinations produce similar
predictions. These similarities motivate \emph{dimensionality reduction}:
representing the predictions with fewer coordinates while retaining their
main variations. The central question is how much detail such a
representation preserves, and where its approximation becomes inadequate.

We investigate this question using a flexible family of synthetic, positive
alignment-response surfaces. Each surface describes the model's response
across spatial scale and redshift, a measure of cosmic expansion. A set of
shape parameters determines each response, which we evaluate on a common
grid. We test how accurately a small number of coordinates can represent
the variation in this response family. We call these \emph{latent
coordinates}, and their number $L$ the \emph{latent dimension}. The model
parameters, sampled grid values and latent coordinates play distinct roles;
Section~\ref{sec:model-sampling} defines their dimensions.

We compare principal component analysis (PCA), which represents surfaces as
linear combinations of shared patterns \citep{2016RSPTA.37450202J}, with a
convolutional autoencoder and a PCA-assisted autoencoder. An autoencoder is a neural network that learns to
encode an input into latent coordinates and reconstruct it from them
\citep{2006Sci...313..504H}. The convolutional model learns patterns along
the wavenumber direction while combining information across redshift. The
PCA-assisted model first projects each surface onto a fixed linear basis,
then learns to compress its coefficients. We compare the three methods at
$L\in\{2,4,6,8,10\}$, using the same training and validation samples.
The evaluation examines difficult surfaces as well as average performance.

PCA and autoencoders are established methods, and neural networks already
have applications in cosmology, including power-spectrum prediction
\citep{2022MNRAS.511.1771S}. This investigation combines the specification of
a flexible alignment-response family, the generation and checking of a
controlled synthetic dataset, and a matched comparison of linear and
nonlinear reconstruction. Assessing where the representations lose accuracy
is part of the investigation, alongside measuring their overall performance.

In the preliminary comparison reported in Section~\ref{sec:results}, the
autoencoder achieves lower reconstruction errors than PCA at the same
latent dimension. The remaining errors show that this improvement alone
does not establish sufficient accuracy for cosmological applications.
The experiment provides a basis for assessing compact alignment
representations before testing their effect on lensing predictions and
inferences from galaxy observations.
