\section{Introduction: the question we ask}
\label{sec:introduction}

Much of the Universe cannot be seen directly.  Astronomers therefore study how
light and matter affect one another, and use those effects as clues.  Weak
gravitational lensing is one such clue: matter slightly changes the apparent
shapes of distant galaxies.  By combining measurements from many galaxies, we
can learn about the distribution of matter and the expansion of the Universe.

The challenge studied here is that galaxy shapes are not random before lensing
occurs.  Galaxies can be influenced by the same surrounding matter and
can therefore develop related orientations.  These \emph{intrinsic alignments} can
mimic or contaminate the weak-lensing signal.  This matters because weak-lensing analyses rely on small statistical patterns
in galaxy shapes, so an additional source of correlated shapes can affect the
interpretation of those patterns. Flexible models of this effect are therefore
useful for describing how intrinsic-alignment contributions may change across
different physical conditions.  In this work, we consider an IA model whose amplitude varies with both physical
scale and redshift. For each choice of model parameters, the model therefore
produces a two-dimensional surface rather than a single number. Our surface is
evaluated on 31 redshift values and 101 wavenumber values, giving
$31\times101=3{,}131$ values for each model realization.  The goal of this project is not to treat these synthetic surfaces as a final
model of intrinsic alignment in real surveys. Instead, they give us a controlled
way to test how well an IA model can be compressed. By changing the model
parameters within the chosen ranges and generating many surfaces, we can test
whether a small number of learned values can preserve most of the information
in the original surface.

\subsection{Research question}

Our main question is:

\begin{quote}
\emph{Can a nonlinear autoencoder compress a 3,131-value intrinsic-alignment
surface into two numbers and reconstruct the surface more accurately than a
linear two-number method?}
\end{quote}

\subsection{Approach}

We answer the question in four stages:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item define a flexible analytic model for how the alignment amplitude
        changes with scale and redshift;
  \item sample its 13 shape parameters and generate 100,000 valid surfaces;
  \item construct a PCA baseline and a convolutional autoencoder using the same
        training and validation split; and
  \item compare reconstruction accuracy, including errors for unusually
        difficult surfaces.
\end{enumerate}
