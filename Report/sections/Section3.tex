\section{Building the intrinsic-alignment model and dataset}
\label{sec:model}

To evaluate compact representations, we require a controlled family of IA
responses with known variation across scale and redshift. We retain the NLA
relations between matter and alignment spectra, then introduce smooth
factors that allow the response to vary within this prescription. Separating
these factors makes their effects interpretable and provides a reproducible
way to generate synthetic surfaces. The resulting flexible IA response
model is phenomenological: we choose functions with specified behaviours
to represent a range of possible responses. These choices define the
reconstruction experiment; they do not constitute a complete theory of
galaxy formation. We explain each factor through its defining equation
and characteristic behaviour, combine the factors into a response surface,
and then describe how the family is sampled.

\subsection{From three-dimensional IA power to the response amplitude}
\label{sec:ia-amplitude}

The angular correlations in Section~\ref{sec:ia-background} are predicted
from three-dimensional spectra. The matter--intrinsic spectrum
$P_{\delta I}$ describes the correlation of matter density with intrinsic
galaxy shape, while $P_{\mathrm{II}}$ describes intrinsic-shape correlations.
In the adopted NLA-inspired prescription,
\begin{equation}
  P_{\delta I}(k,z)=\mathcal A_{\rm IA}(k,z)P_{\delta\delta}^{\mathrm{NL}}(k,z),
  \qquad
  P_{\mathrm{II}}(k,z)=\mathcal A_{\rm IA}^{\,2}(k,z)P_{\delta\delta}^{\mathrm{NL}}(k,z).
  \label{eq:nla-power-spectra}
\end{equation}
Here $P_{\delta\delta}^{\mathrm{NL}}(k,z)$ is the nonlinear matter power spectrum.
The comoving wavenumber $k$ specifies spatial scale, with larger $k$
corresponding to smaller scales, and $z$ is redshift.
The response enters once when one member of the correlation is an
intrinsic shape, and twice when both are. A survey prediction projects
these spectra along the line of sight, with the appropriate galaxy
distributions and lensing geometry, to obtain the angular $\mathrm{GI}$,
$\mathrm{IG}$ and $\mathrm{II}$ contributions
\citep{2007NJPh....9..444B}.

We separate the signed IA amplitude into
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal A_{\rm IA}(k,z)
  = -A_0\,\mathcal A_\Omega(z)\,\mathcal A_\Theta(k,z).
  \label{eq:ia_decomposition}
\end{equation}
The constant $A_0$ sets the overall strength, and the cosmological factor is
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal A_\Omega(z)=C_{\rm NLA}\frac{\Omega_{\rm m}}{D(z)},
  \label{eq:a-omega}
\end{equation}
where $\Omega_{\rm m}$ is the present matter density divided by the present
critical density, and $D(z)$ is the linear growth factor, normalized to
$D(0)=1$.
The dimensionless normalization $C_{\rm NLA}=0.0134$ includes the product
usually written $C_1\rho_{\rm crit}$, with $\rho_{\rm crit}$ the present
critical density. The inverse-growth dependence is part of the adopted
NLA prescription: as $D(z)$ decreases towards higher redshift,
$\mathcal A_\Omega$ increases. This factor is computed from the background
cosmology and is separate from the sampled shape parameters.

The positive response surface is
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal A_\Theta(k,z)=R_z(z)R_L(z)S(k,z)>0.
  \label{eq:shape_surface}
\end{equation}
The factor $R_z$ allows a broad redshift trend, $R_L$ represents an
additional trend motivated by luminosity and population changes, and $S$
describes scale dependence. Their product supplies the family of surfaces
used to compare PCA and autoencoder reconstruction.

We compress $\log_{10}\mathcal A_\Theta$. This separates reconstruction of
the flexible response from the external normalization $A_0$ and the
prescribed cosmological factor. Amplitude variation across the surface is
still retained. Section~\ref{sec:autoencoder} defines the input
transformation and normalization.

\subsection{The broad redshift factor \texorpdfstring{$R_z$}{Rz}}
\label{sec:redshift-factor}

The inverse-growth factor alone need not describe how the alignment of an
observed galaxy population evolves. We allow residual evolution relative
to a fixed pivot $z_*=0.5$ by defining
\begin{equation}
  r(z)=\frac{1+z}{1+z_*},
  \qquad R_z(z)=r^\eta(z).
  \label{eq:broad-redshift-factor}
\end{equation}
The ratio $r$ is dimensionless and equals one at the pivot, so
$R_z(z_*)=1$ for every $\eta$. The pivot therefore supplies a common
normalization rather than a preferred transition in the evolution.

Figure~\ref{fig:ia-eta} illustrates the effect of $\eta$. Positive values
give a larger response above the pivot and a smaller one below it; negative
values reverse this trend. At $\eta=0$, $R_z=1$. Because $R_z$ depends only
on redshift, it rescales the response uniformly across wavenumber at each
fixed $z$.

  $\eta\in\{-1,-0.5,0,+0.5,+1\}$. The dotted vertical line marks

\subsection{The luminosity factor \texorpdfstring{$R_L$}{RL}}
\label{sec:luminosity-factor}

A survey's luminosity distribution and population mix can evolve with
redshift, changing the alignment response of the observed galaxies.
Multiplying $R_z$ by a second power law would only change its overall
exponent. We instead introduce a smooth transition, allowing a change in
the rate of evolution that a single power law cannot describe. The
luminosity factor $R_L(z)$ represents this additional population-dependent
trend and shares the normalization pivot of $R_z$. We do not fit a survey
luminosity function here.

Three parameters describe this factor: $z_q$ locates the transition,
$\xi$ controls the direction and strength of the additional evolution,
and $s>0$ controls the transition sharpness. With
$r_q=(1+z_q)/(1+z_*)$, we choose
\begin{equation}
  R_L(z)=
  \left[
    \frac{1+[r(z)/r_q]^s}{1+(1/r_q)^s}
  \right]^{\xi/s}.
  \label{eq:luminosity-factor}
\end{equation}
The numerator changes smoothly as $r/r_q=(1+z)/(1+z_q)$ passes through
unity. At $z=z_*$, the numerator equals the denominator, giving
$R_L(z_*)=1$. This normalization separates evolution relative to the pivot
from the overall amplitude.

Figure~\ref{fig:ia-luminosity} illustrates these three controls in the
order $z_q$, $\xi$ and $s$.

\begin{figure}[!htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{ia_luminosity_factors_panels.pdf}
  \caption{Luminosity factor $R_L(z)$ with one parameter varied in each
  panel. (a) Transition redshift
  $z_q\in\{0.5,1.0,1.5,2.0,2.5\}$ at $\xi=1$ and $s=4$.
  (b) Evolution index $\xi\in\{-2,-1,0,+1,+2\}$ at $s=2$ and $z_q=1.5$.
  (c) Sharpness $s\in\{1,2,4,6,8\}$ at $\xi=1$ and $z_q=1.5$.
  The pivot and unit guides have the same meaning as in
  Figure~\ref{fig:ia-eta}. The larger sharpness in panel (a) makes the
  transition more visible. The illustrative $\xi=\pm2$ curves extend beyond
  the dataset's sampling interval; the black curve in panel (b) has
  $\xi=0$ and hence $R_L=1$.}
  \label{fig:ia-luminosity}
\end{figure}

In panel (a), increasing $z_q$ moves the transition to higher redshift,
corresponding to earlier cosmic time. Since every curve passes through
$R_L=1$ at $z_*=0.5$, moving the transition also changes the response
relative to that fixed reference.

In panel (b), positive $\xi$ increases $R_L$ above the pivot, negative
$\xi$ decreases it, and $\xi=0$ gives unity at every redshift. In this
last case there is no luminosity transition, and $z_q$ and $s$ have no
effect on the response.

In panel (c), smaller $s$ spreads the transition over a broader redshift
interval, while larger $s$ concentrates it around $z_q$. Far above the
transition, $R_L$ approaches a power law in $1+z$ with exponent $\xi$.
Changing $s$ can also change the normalization of that trend, even though
its limiting exponent stays the same. The plotted interval need not reach
this limit; its visible curvature is the additional freedom supplied by
$R_L$.

The distinction between these controls can also be seen from the local
logarithmic slope,
\begin{equation}
  \frac{d\ln(R_zR_L)}{d\ln(1+z)}
  =\eta+\xi\frac{[(1+z)/(1+z_q)]^s}{1+[(1+z)/(1+z_q)]^s}.
  \label{eq:population-evolution-slope}
\end{equation}
The broad contribution is $\eta$, while the additional contribution
changes smoothly towards $\xi$ and equals $\xi/2$ at $z=z_q$.
Consequently, $\eta$ and $\xi$ can partly compensate over a limited
redshift interval. The plots illustrate distinct controls, but do not
imply that observations could determine all of them independently.

\subsection{The joint scale factor \texorpdfstring{$S(k,z)$}{S(k,z)}}
\label{sec:scale-factor}

The redshift-only factors change the response uniformly across spatial
scales. They cannot describe a small-scale alignment response that differs
from its large-scale behaviour. We therefore introduce a smooth correction
$S(k,z)$ that approaches unity on large scales and can vary at higher
wavenumber. It modifies the IA response; the nonlinear evolution of the
matter spectrum is already included in $P_{\delta\delta}^{\mathrm{NL}}(k,z)$.

The scale response has a transition wavenumber $k_t$, an onset sharpness
$n$, a high-wavenumber correction index $\alpha$, and a tail-curvature
parameter $m$.
We allow these quantities to evolve smoothly through the same redshift
ratio $r(z)$:
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
    k_t(z)&=k_{t,*}\,r^{\gamma_t}(z),&
    n(z)&=n_*\,r^{\gamma_n}(z),\\
    \alpha(z)&=\alpha\,r^{\gamma_\alpha}(z),&
    m(z)&=m\,r^{\gamma_m}(z).
  \end{aligned}
  \label{eq:scale-parameter-evolution}
\end{equation}
The constants $k_{t,*}$, $n_*$, $\alpha$ and $m$ are the values at
$z_*$. Each $\gamma$ controls evolution away from that pivot; setting it
to zero makes the corresponding quantity constant. This evolution keeps
$k_t$, $n$ and $m$ positive and preserves the sign of $\alpha$ for the
adopted parameter ranges.

Using the dimensionless ratio $x=k/k_t(z)$, we define
\begin{equation}
  S(k,z)=1+q\,
  \frac{x^{n(z)}}{1+x^{n(z)}}
  \left(\frac{1+x^{m(z)}}{2}\right)^{\alpha(z)/m(z)}.
  \label{eq:scale-response}
\end{equation}
The first factor in the correction, $x^n/(1+x^n)$, rises smoothly from
zero to one and controls its onset. The second factor adjusts the
high-wavenumber behaviour, while $q$ sets the strength of the correction.

Three simple properties clarify the construction:
\begin{equation}
  \begin{gathered}
    S(k,z)\longrightarrow1\quad\text{as }k\longrightarrow0
    \text{ at fixed }z,\\
    q=0\ \Longrightarrow\ S(k,z)=1,
    \qquad S(k_t(z),z)=1+\frac{q}{2}.
  \end{gathered}
  \label{eq:scale-reference-limits}
\end{equation}
The first recovers the large-scale baseline, and the second removes all
scale dependence. For the transition value, substituting $k=k_t(z)$
gives $x=1$: the onset factor is $1/2$ and the tail factor is one. This
explains the division by two in Equation~\eqref{eq:scale-response}.
At fixed redshift, for $q>0$ and $k\gg k_t(z)$, the correction follows
$S-1\propto[k/k_t(z)]^{\alpha(z)}$. The exponent therefore describes
the high-$k$ correction, rather than the full factor including its unit
baseline.

Figure~\ref{fig:shape-surface} shows a fiducial example with positive
$\alpha$. The large-scale region remains close to $S=1$, while the
correction increases towards smaller scales. Evolution of the scale
parameters allows the position and shape of this pattern to vary with
redshift.

  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.80\linewidth]{ia_scale_redshift_surface.pdf}
  \caption{Fiducial scale--redshift factor $S(k,z)$ on the model grid.  The

  $k_{t,*}=0.5\,{\rm Mpc}^{-1}$, $q=1$, $n_*=2$, $\alpha=0.3$, and $m=2$.
  Their evolution uses $\gamma_t=0.4$, $\gamma_n=0.2$ and
  $\gamma_\alpha=\gamma_m=0$. The response approaches unity at low $k$
  and increases at high $k$ for this fiducial choice.}
  \label{fig:shape-surface}
\end{figure}

The five scale controls have the following roles:
\begin{description}[style=unboxed,leftmargin=0pt,itemsep=0.5em,parsep=0pt]
  \item[Correction strength, $q$.]
  This parameter multiplies the scale-dependent contribution, with
  Equation~\eqref{eq:scale-reference-limits} relating it to the response at
  the transition. When the correction is absent, the remaining scale
  parameters have no effect.

  \item[Transition scale, $k_t(z)$.]
  The ratio $k/k_t$ locates the transition from the large-scale baseline.
  Increasing $k_t$ moves the transition to smaller comoving scales.
  Positive $\gamma_t$ places it at higher wavenumber above the pivot.
  This is a model transition, not a measured boundary between physical
  regimes.

  \item[Transition sharpness, $n(z)$.]
  Smaller $n$ makes the onset factor change over a broader range of
  scales; larger $n$ concentrates that change near $k_t$. The tail
  factor determines how the correction continues beyond this onset.

  \item[High-$k$ behaviour, $\alpha(z)$.]
  Positive values produce a growing high-$k$ correction, while negative
  values make it decay towards zero at sufficiently high $k$, returning
  $S$ towards unity. At $\alpha=0$, the tail factor is one and
  $S\longrightarrow1+q$ as $k$ increases.

  \item[Tail curvature, $m(z)$.]
  This parameter controls how smoothly the tail factor approaches its
  high-$k$ trend and can change the normalization of that trend.
  It complements $n$, which controls the onset factor. When
  $\alpha=0$, both $m$ and its evolution index have no effect.
\end{description}

The sampled range uses $q\geq0$. Both factors multiplying $q$ are
positive, so $S\geq1$: the scale correction enhances the response relative
to the redshift-only baseline. A negative $\alpha$ reduces this enhancement
at sufficiently high $k$ but does not produce suppression below the
baseline. This restriction defines the scope of the response family.
The controls describe features of a surface rather than independently
measured galaxy properties. Their effects can overlap or disappear in the
limiting cases above, motivating a search for compact representations.

These limits also give testable reasons for unequal parameter sensitivity.
When $q$ is small, changing the other scale parameters has little effect
on the full surface. When $\alpha$ is close to zero, the tail-curvature
parameters are weakly expressed. Such overlaps can make approximate
compression efficient without reducing the exact number of parameters
that define the model family.

\subsection{The combined shape amplitude and the IA power spectra}
\label{sec:combined-amplitude}

Multiplying $R_z$, $R_L$ and $S$ gives the response surface that is later
compressed. All three factors are positive for the adopted parameter
ranges, so $\mathcal A_\Theta>0$ and its logarithm is defined throughout
the grid. Section~\ref{sec:autoencoder} explains this input transformation.
The pivot normalization fixes $R_z(z_*)=R_L(z_*)=1$, so the combined
response there is $\mathcal A_\Theta(k,z_*)=S(k,z_*)$, which need not
equal unity. The separate
normalization $A_0$ and cosmological factor $\mathcal A_\Omega$ can then
be applied to recover the signed amplitude in
Equation~\eqref{eq:ia_decomposition}.

Figure~\ref{fig:ia-theta-surface} shows the product of the three factors
and connects their separate controls to variation across the full grid.

  \centering

  \caption{Fiducial shape amplitude $\mathcal A_\Theta(k,z)=R_z R_L S$ on
  the same $(k,z)$ grid as Figure~\ref{fig:shape-surface}.  The horizontal
  axis is $\log_{10}(k/{\rm Mpc}^{-1})$ and the vertical axis is redshift.
  Colour is the value of $\mathcal A_\Theta$.  The fiducial luminosity

  \label{fig:ia-theta-surface}

At fixed redshift, this fiducial surface increases towards small scales
because $S$ increases with $k$. Its vertical variation combines $R_z$
with the redshift evolution of the scale parameters. The choice $\xi=0$
isolates these effects; the nontrivial luminosity trends are illustrated
separately in Figure~\ref{fig:ia-luminosity}. Multiplication by
$-A_0\mathcal A_\Omega(z)$ then gives the signed IA amplitude in
Equation~\eqref{eq:ia_decomposition}.

Figure~\ref{fig:ia-power-spectra} shows how this amplitude enters the
three-dimensional spectra through Equation~\eqref{eq:nla-power-spectra}.
The matter spectrum is determined independently of the IA response, while
the two alignment spectra inherit its scale and redshift dependence.

In panel (a), matter power is lower at higher redshift because structure
has grown less. The curves generally decrease with increasing $k$ over
the plotted interval. Nonlinear enhancement refers to a comparison with
linear matter power at the same scale; Figure~\ref{fig:ia-power-spectra}
does not display that comparison.

Panel (b) scales matter power by one factor of $\mathcal A_{\rm IA}$.
Where $S\simeq1$, this is approximately a redshift-dependent rescaling.
At smaller scales, the fiducial correction increases

Panel (c) contains two response factors, so this relative enhancement is
stronger. The redshift ordering also changes between panels because the
IA amplitude evolves as well as the matter spectrum. These are properties
of the illustrated parameter choice; the sampled response family allows
other trends.

\subsection{Sampling the response family and constructing the dataset}
\label{sec:model-sampling}

We collect the shape parameters listed in Table~\ref{tab:sampling-bounds}
into the vector $\bm{\Theta}$, with $N_\Theta=13$ entries. They control
redshift and luminosity evolution, the scale response, and its redshift
dependence. The external normalization $A_0$ and the cosmological factor
$\mathcal A_\Omega$ are excluded from this vector and are not sampled.
Sampling combinations of these controls provides known target surfaces
for a comparison of compression methods, including responses that differ
substantially from the fiducial example.

Table~\ref{tab:sampling-bounds} defines the sampling distributions. These
ranges specify the scope of the reconstruction experiment, providing
varied responses while limiting exceptionally large variations within a
surface. The transition wavenumber is sampled log-uniformly, assigning
equal probability to equal multiplicative intervals; the other controls
are sampled uniformly in their listed coordinates, independently of one
another. We generate 100,000
surfaces on $N_z=31$ equally spaced redshifts from $z=0$ to $z=3$ and
$N_k=101$ logarithmically spaced wavenumbers from $k=0.01$ to
$10\,\mathrm{Mpc}^{-1}$. Each surface is therefore an $N_z\times N_k$
array, with redshift along the first axis and wavenumber along the second.

Before fixing these bounds, we evaluated 8,192 random parameter
combinations and all $2^{N_\Theta}=8{,}192$ corners of the parameter domain.
Every evaluated surface was finite and positive. The corner checks
examine simultaneous extreme parameter choices that random sampling may
miss. These checks also test whether the values remain within the
computer's numerical range across the model grid; they do not
establish observational validity or exhaust every combination within the
parameter ranges.

We also checked that the chosen bounds retain varied surfaces without
allowing a few extreme combinations to dominate their amplitude range.
For a surface, define its internal range as the difference between the
largest and smallest $\log_{10}\mathcal A_\Theta$ values, in dex. The
99th percentile of this range over the random checks is $1.656$ dex,
below the design ceiling of $1.75$ dex; the largest corner range is
$2.958$ dex, below a $3.00$ dex ceiling. The median, over grid points,
of the random ensemble's 1st--99th percentile log-amplitude spread is
$1.025$ dex, above a $1.00$ dex diversity reference. These are numerical
design criteria for this experiment, not physical bounds derived from data.

Figure~\ref{fig:sampling-ensemble} shows the distribution of the random
range-check surfaces. Their spread illustrates why reconstruction should
be assessed across the family, including surfaces with large internal
variation, rather than only near a single fiducial response.

The stored dataset is divided into 80,000 training surfaces, 10,000
validation surfaces and 10,000 test surfaces. Training determines the PCA
basis, normalization statistics and network parameters. Validation is used
to monitor training and compare architecture, latent-width and training
choices. The test subset is reserved for the final performance evaluation
after these choices are frozen. Fitting preprocessing steps only on the
training subset prevents information from the held-out surfaces from
entering those steps.

Each parameter vector determines all $N_zN_k$ grid values, so those
values are not independent physical parameters. The
reconstruction problem is to represent the variation across this family
with a short latent vector at an acceptable approximation error. The
required latent dimension is an empirical result of that comparison; it
need not equal $N_\Theta$.
