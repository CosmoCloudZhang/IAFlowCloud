\section{Building the intrinsic-alignment model and dataset}
\label{sec:model}

Section~\ref{sec:background} introduced intrinsic alignment as the physical
effect we want to represent.  This section turns that problem into a
controlled dataset for compression.  We first separate the overall amplitude
from the shape of the response, then define the redshift and scale dependence
of that shape, sample the model parameters, and finally construct the
training, validation, and test sets used in the later PCA and autoencoder
comparisons.

\subsection{Separating the overall amplitude from the surface shape}

We write the signed intrinsic-alignment amplitude as

\begin{equation}
  \mathcal A_{\rm IA}(k,z)
  = -A_0\,\mathcal A_\Omega(z)\,\mathcal A_\Theta(k,z).
  \label{eq:ia_decomposition}
\end{equation}

Here $A_0$ is an overall multiplier, $\mathcal A_\Omega(z)$ contains the
standard cosmological redshift dependence, and $\mathcal A_\Theta(k,z)>0$ is
the flexible shape surface studied in this project.  We compress
$\log_{10}\mathcal A_\Theta$, not the signed full amplitude.  Keeping $A_0$
outside the 13-dimensional shape model means that the autoencoder is tested
on the variation in the shape of the response rather than on a trivial change
in its overall normalization.

\subsection{Redshift and luminosity factors}

Let

\begin{equation}
  r(z)=\frac{1+z}{1+z_*},
  \qquad
  r_q=\frac{1+z_q}{1+z_*},
\end{equation}

where $z_*=0.5$ is a fixed pivot redshift.  The broad redshift response and the
smooth luminosity-like transition are

\begin{align}
  R_z(z) &= r(z)^\eta,\\
  R_L(z) &=
  \left[
    \frac{1+[r(z)/r_q]^s}{1+(1/r_q)^s}
  \right]^{\xi/s}.
  \label{eq:redshift_luminosity}
\end{align}

The parameter $\eta$ tilts the overall redshift trend.  The parameter $\xi$
sets the strength and direction of the second trend, $z_q$ locates its
transition, and $s$ controls the transition sharpness.  These factors describe
redshift evolution before any explicit scale dependence is introduced.

\subsection{Scale dependence and its redshift evolution}

We use the scale-dependent factor to give the model enough freedom to produce
different shapes across $k$, while allowing the location and sharpness of
these features to change with redshift.  The characteristic scale and shape
parameters evolve as

\begin{align}
  k_t(z) &= k_{t,*}\,r(z)^{\gamma_t}, &
  n(z) &= n_*\,r(z)^{\gamma_n},\\
  \alpha(z) &= \alpha\,r(z)^{\gamma_\alpha}, &
  m(z) &= m\,r(z)^{\gamma_m}.
\end{align}

With $x=k/k_t(z)$, the scale response is

\begin{equation}
  S(k,z)=1+q\,
  \frac{x^{n(z)}}{1+x^{n(z)}}
  \left(\frac{1+x^{m(z)}}{2}\right)^{\alpha(z)/m(z)}.
  \label{eq:scale_response}
\end{equation}

The purpose is not to reproduce one particular observed IA curve, but to
create a controlled family with different kinds of scale and redshift
dependence.

The full positive surface is

\begin{equation}
  \mathcal A_\Theta(k,z)=R_z(z)R_L(z)S(k,z).
  \label{eq:shape_surface}
\end{equation}

Each parameter controls a distinct feature: $k_{t,*}$ moves the transition
along the scale axis, $q$ changes the size of the response, $n_*$ controls the
transition sharpness, and $\alpha$ changes the high-$k$ slope.  The four
$\gamma$ parameters allow these features to evolve with redshift.

\subsection{Sampling, physical checks, and the data grid}

We draw 100,000 parameter combinations from the ranges in
Table~\ref{tab:priors}.  Each combination produces a surface on 31 redshift
points from $z=0$ to $z=3$ and 101 logarithmically spaced wavenumbers from
$k=0.01$ to $10\,\mathrm{Mpc}^{-1}$.  Before fixing these ranges, we evaluated
8,192 random draws and all $2^{13}=8{,}192$ corners of the parameter box.  All
of those surfaces were finite and positive.  Dynamic-range checks were used
to keep the family broad enough for compression tests without allowing
unmanageably extreme corner cases.

\subsection{Training, validation, and test subsets}

The stored split contains 80,000 training surfaces, 10,000 validation surfaces,
and 10,000 test surfaces.  The training subset is used to fit the PCA basis
and the autoencoder, while the validation subset is used for model selection
and diagnostics.  The test subset remains unused until the method has been
chosen.  Normalization statistics and the PCA basis are calculated from
training data only.

This is a phenomenological model family.  The sampled ranges are documented
data-generation bounds, not observational constraints, and the surfaces have
not been calibrated to a particular galaxy survey.  The resulting dataset
therefore gives us a controlled test of compression: every surface has the
same $(k,z)$ grid, but the underlying parameters vary across the prescribed
13-dimensional space.  In the next section, we use this dataset to test
whether the $3{,}131$ grid values can be represented by a much smaller latent
vector.
