\section{Building the intrinsic-alignment model and dataset}
\label{sec:model}

Section~\ref{sec:background} introduced intrinsic alignment as the effect we
need to represent.  Here that representation becomes a dataset.  We first
separate the overall amplitude from the shape of the response, then introduce
the redshift and scale factors, and only afterwards sample the 13-parameter
family and reserve independent subsets for fitting and evaluation.

\subsection{From three-dimensional IA power to the response amplitude}

The GI and II correlations of Section~\ref{sec:ia-background} have
three-dimensional NLA ingredients
\[
  P_{\delta I}(k,z)=\mathcal A_{\rm IA}(k,z)P_\delta^{\rm nl}(k,z),
  \qquad
  P_{II}(k,z)=\mathcal A_{\rm IA}^{\,2}(k,z)P_\delta^{\rm nl}(k,z).
\]
The spectrum $P_\delta^{\rm nl}(k,z)$, also written
$P_{\delta\delta}^{\rm nl}$, is the nonlinear matter power.  Both IA spectra
are model ingredients rather than catalogue measurements; a survey prediction
projects them along the line of sight to recover the angular GI, IG, and II
terms discussed in Section~\ref{sec:ia-background}
\citep{2007NJPh....9..444B}.

The signed response is written
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal A_{\rm IA}(k,z)
  = -A_0\,\mathcal A_\Omega(z)\,\mathcal A_\Theta(k,z).
  \label{eq:ia_decomposition}
\end{equation}
The constant $A_0$ sets the overall IA strength.  The remaining cosmological
factor is the standard NLA term
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal A_\Omega(z)=C_1\frac{\Omega_{\rm m}}{D(z)},
  \label{eq:a-omega}
\end{equation}
with $C_1=0.0134$ and with $D(z)$ the linear growth factor, normalized so that
$D(0)=1$.  Growth is smaller at earlier times, so $\mathcal A_\Omega$
increases with redshift: a given tidal field produces a larger alignment
response when structure is less grown.  This factor is computed from the
background cosmology.  It is not one of the 13 sampled shape parameters.

What remains is the positive surface later passed to PCA and the autoencoder,
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal A_\Theta(k,z)=R_z(z)R_L(z)S(k,z)>0.
  \label{eq:shape_surface}
\end{equation}
We compress $\log_{10}\mathcal A_\Theta$ rather than the signed full
amplitude, so the test concerns variation across the surface rather than a
single overall multiplier.  The three factors have distinct jobs: $R_z$
supplies a broad redshift trend, $R_L$ is a luminosity factor, and $S$
carries the scale dependence.  Their product, and the power spectra built
from it, are shown in Section~\ref{sec:combined-amplitude}.

\subsection{The broad redshift factor \texorpdfstring{$R_z$}{Rz}}

A lensing survey sees galaxies at many cosmic times, so a redshift-independent
IA response would be too narrow.  We therefore measure evolution relative to a
fixed pivot $z_*=0.5$.  The factor $R_z$ is a power law in $1+z$, controlled
by a single index $\eta$.  It tilts the whole redshift interval up or down
and does not introduce a preferred intermediate redshift.  The exact
definition is given in Appendix~\ref{app:detailed-equations}.

\begin{figure}[!htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.62\linewidth]{ia_eta_panel.pdf}
  \caption{Broad redshift factor $R_z(z)$ while $\eta$ is varied.
  The horizontal axis is redshift $z$ and the vertical axis is $R_z$.
  The dotted vertical line marks the pivot $z_*=0.5$, and the dashed
  horizontal line marks the normalization value $1$.  Every curve equals
  unity at that pivot.  The curves use
  $\eta\in\{-1,-0.5,0,+0.5,+1\}$.  Redder curves have more negative $\eta$
  and bluer curves more positive $\eta$; the black curve is $\eta=0$.}
  \label{fig:ia-eta}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:ia-eta} shows how $\eta$ changes the IA shape amplitude.
When $\eta$ is positive, $R_z$---and therefore $\mathcal A_\Theta$---is
larger above the pivot and smaller below it.  A negative index reverses the
tilt, and $\eta=0$ leaves $R_z=1$.  Because $R_z$ multiplies the entire
$(k,z)$ surface at a given redshift, this parameter rescales the IA
amplitude uniformly across wavenumber.

\subsection{The luminosity factor \texorpdfstring{$R_L$}{RL}}
\label{sec:luminosity-factor}

A single broad tilt cannot describe a response that changes character around
an intermediate redshift.  The galaxy sample itself also changes: its
effective luminosity and mix of populations evolve, and that evolution can
imitate an extra trend in the alignment amplitude.  The luminosity factor
$R_L$ is a redshift-only phenomenological stand-in for that effect.  It is
not calibrated to a survey luminosity function.

Three parameters set its shape.  The sharpness $s$ decides how rapidly the
change occurs, the amplitude $\xi$ controls its strength and direction, and
$z_q$ locates the change in slope.  The exact definition is given in
Appendix~\ref{app:detailed-equations}.

\begin{figure}[!htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{ia_luminosity_factors_panels.pdf}
  \caption{Luminosity factor $R_L(z)$, each panel varied one parameter at a
  time.  Axes, pivot line, and unit line have the same meaning as in
  Figure~\ref{fig:ia-eta}.  Panel (a) varies the sharpness
  $s\in\{1,2,4,6,8\}$ at $\xi=1$ and $z_q=1.5$.  Panel (b) varies the
  strength $\xi\in\{-2,-1,0,+1,+2\}$ at $s=2$ and $z_q=1.5$; redder curves
  are more negative $\xi$ and bluer curves more positive $\xi$, and the
  black curve is $\xi=0$, which removes $R_L$ entirely.  Panel (c) varies
  the transition redshift $z_q\in\{0.5,1.0,1.5,2.0,2.5\}$ at $\xi=1$ and
  $s=4$.  The larger sharpness is used only to make the knee easier to see.
  Every curve equals $1$ at $z_*=0.5$.}
  \label{fig:ia-luminosity}
\end{figure}
\FloatBarrier

In Figure~\ref{fig:ia-luminosity}(a), a small $s$ spreads the same change
over a wide redshift range, so $R_L$ looks almost like another gradual tilt;
a large $s$ concentrates it into a narrow window around $z_q$.  The
sharpness does not move the high-redshift destination.

In Figure~\ref{fig:ia-luminosity}(b), positive $\xi$ raises $R_L$---and the
IA shape amplitude---above the pivot, negative $\xi$ lowers it, and $\xi=0$
returns $R_L=1$.

The location $z_q$ in Figure~\ref{fig:ia-luminosity}(c) decides when the
change arrives.  Increasing $z_q$ pushes the knee to later cosmic time: the
curve remains closer to its low-redshift branch across more of the plotted
interval, and only later turns toward the high-redshift slope set by $\xi$.
A smaller $z_q$ brings the same turn-on earlier.  Because every curve is
forced through $R_L=1$ at $z_*=0.5$, moving $z_q$ also changes how much of
the transition lies above the pivot.

\subsection{The joint scale factor \texorpdfstring{$S(k,z)$}{S(k,z)}}

Both $R_z$ and $R_L$ depend only on redshift.  Nonlinear scales bring an
extra uncertainty that has to vary with $k$ as well, and that role is given
to $S(k,z)$.

\begin{figure}[!htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.80\linewidth]{ia_scale_redshift_surface.pdf}
  \caption{Fiducial scale--redshift factor $S(k,z)$ on the model grid.  The
  horizontal axis is $\log_{10}(k/{\rm Mpc}^{-1})$, so the left side of the
  panel is large-scale modes and the right side is small-scale modes.  The
  vertical axis is redshift from $z=0$ to $z=3$.  Colour is the value of
  $S$ itself.  At $z_*=0.5$ the fiducial choices are
  $k_{t,*}=0.5\,{\rm Mpc}^{-1}$, $q=1$, $n_*=2$, $\alpha=0.3$, and $m=2$.
  Low $k$ stays close to the baseline $S=1$; the warmer colours at higher
  $k$ are the scale correction turning on.}
  \label{fig:shape-surface}
\end{figure}
\FloatBarrier

Figure~\ref{fig:shape-surface} stays near $S=1$ on large scales.  The
correction appears at higher $k$, and the vertical direction lets that
scale response itself change with cosmic time.  The parameters that build
this surface are separated below; their complete combination is given in
Appendix~\ref{app:detailed-equations}.

\subsubsection{The scale-correction strength \texorpdfstring{$q$}{q}}

The parameter $q$ sets how large the scale-dependent correction is.  When
$q=0$ the correction vanishes and $S=1$.  Larger values raise
$\mathcal A_\Theta$ at $k\gtrsim k_t$ while leaving the largest scales
almost unchanged.

\subsubsection{The transition scale \texorpdfstring{$k_t(z)$}{kt(z)}}

Not every wavenumber should feel that correction equally.  The scale
$k_t(z)$ marks where it begins to turn on: modes with $k\ll k_t$ remain
near the large-scale baseline, while $k\gtrsim k_t$ is increasingly affected
by $S$.  Moving $k_t$ to larger $k$ postpones the rise in the IA amplitude
until smaller physical scales.  The particular transition used here is a
construction of this project, not a measured physical boundary.  Its pivot
value $k_{t,*}$ fixes the location at $z_*=0.5$, and $\gamma_t$ allows that
location to move with redshift.

\subsubsection{The transition sharpness \texorpdfstring{$n(z)$}{n(z)}}

The same onset can be gradual or abrupt.  Smaller $n$ spreads the turn-on
across a broad range of scales, so the IA amplitude leaves the baseline well
below $k_t$ and finishes the rise only well above it.  Larger $n$
concentrates most of the change near $k_t$.  The pivot value $n_*$ sets this
sharpness at $z_*=0.5$, and $\gamma_n$ lets the width evolve.

\subsubsection{The high-\texorpdfstring{$k$}{k} slope
\texorpdfstring{$\alpha(z)$}{alpha(z)}}

Once the correction is on, the small-scale tail need not be flat.  The
parameter $\alpha(z)$ sets that high-$k$ trend: positive values let the IA
shape amplitude keep rising toward smaller scales, negative values let it
fall, and $\alpha=0$ adds no extra tilt.  The pivot value $\alpha$ is the
slope at $z_*=0.5$, and $\gamma_\alpha$ controls its redshift evolution.

\subsubsection{The tail smoothness \texorpdfstring{$m(z)$}{m(z)}}

The high-$k$ slope still has to join the transition without a corner.  The
parameter $m(z)$ sets that curvature.  It is kept separate from $n(z)$
because $n$ controls how the correction switches on, whereas $m$ controls the
shape of the tail afterward.  The pivot value $m$ and the index $\gamma_m$
specify this behaviour across redshift.

The pivot parameters therefore describe the scale response at $z_*$, and the
four $\gamma$ indices describe how those controls evolve.  Their meaning is
operational---strength, onset scale, transition width, tail slope, and tail
curvature---rather than a claim that each one is an independently measured
galaxy property.

\subsection{The combined shape amplitude and the IA power spectra}
\label{sec:combined-amplitude}

Multiplying the three factors gives the surface that is later compressed.
Figure~\ref{fig:ia-theta-surface} shows that product for the same fiducial
choices used above.

\begin{figure}[!htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.80\linewidth]{ia_theta_surface.pdf}
  \caption{Fiducial shape amplitude $\mathcal A_\Theta(k,z)=R_z R_L S$ on
  the same $(k,z)$ grid as Figure~\ref{fig:shape-surface}.  The horizontal
  axis is $\log_{10}(k/{\rm Mpc}^{-1})$ and the vertical axis is redshift.
  Colour is the value of $\mathcal A_\Theta$.  The fiducial luminosity
  parameters are $\xi=0$, $s=2$, and $z_q=1$, so $R_L=1$ and the extra
  redshift trend comes only from $\eta=0.5$.  Large-scale columns on the
  left are close to that redshift-only amplitude.  Small-scale columns on
  the right are larger because the fiducial $S$ has turned on.}
  \label{fig:ia-theta-surface}
\end{figure}
\FloatBarrier

At fixed redshift the surface rises toward small scales because $S$ increases
with $k$.  At fixed wavenumber the vertical trend follows $R_z$, $R_L$, and
the slow redshift drift of the scale parameters.  The fiducial point shown
here has $R_L=1$, so that trend is mainly the $\eta=0.5$ tilt.  The signed
response is this surface times $-A_0\mathcal A_\Omega(z)$, which adds a
further increase with redshift and does not depend on $k$.

The same amplitude then enters the three-dimensional spectra,
$P_{\delta I}=\mathcal A_{\rm IA}P_\delta^{\rm nl}$ and
$P_{II}=\mathcal A_{\rm IA}^{2}P_\delta^{\rm nl}$.
Figure~\ref{fig:ia-power-spectra} shows the result at five redshifts.

\begin{figure}[!htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{ia_power_spectra_panels.pdf}
  \caption{The three power spectra used in the NLA construction.  The
  horizontal axis in every panel is wavenumber $k$ on a logarithmic scale,
  from $0.01$ to $10\,{\rm Mpc}^{-1}$.  The vertical axes are the spectra
  in ${\rm Mpc}^{3}$, also logarithmic.  Colour is redshift:
  $z=0$, $0.5$, $1$, $2$, and $3$ run from red to blue.  Panel (a) is the
  nonlinear matter spectrum $P_\delta^{\rm nl}\equiv P_{\delta\delta}^{\rm nl}$,
  which does not depend on $\mathcal A_{\rm IA}$.  Panel (b) shows
  $-P_{\delta I}$ because the adopted sign convention makes
  $P_{\delta I}<0$; the signed values, rather than their negatives, enter
  the model.  Panel (c) shows the positive $P_{II}$.  The curves use the
  notebook's Planck-like parameters and the fiducial IA response, with an
  Eisenstein--Hu transfer function and Halofit nonlinear correction in
  PyCCL.  They illustrate the model relations and are not a fit to survey
  data.}
  \label{fig:ia-power-spectra}
\end{figure}
\FloatBarrier

The matter spectrum in panel (a) is independent of $\mathcal A_{\rm IA}$.  It
falls with redshift because structure is less grown at earlier times, and it
rises from large scales into a small-scale nonlinear enhancement.  Panel (b)
is that spectrum multiplied by $\mathcal A_{\rm IA}$.  On large scales
$S\approx 1$, so $P_{\delta I}$ is essentially a redshift-dependent rescaling
of $P_{\delta\delta}^{\rm nl}$.  On small scales the fiducial $S$ exceeds
unity, and $|P_{\delta I}|/P_{\delta\delta}^{\rm nl}$ therefore grows with
$k$.  The redshift ordering also differs from panel (a), because
$\mathcal A_\Omega(z)R_z(z)$ itself evolves.  Panel (c) multiplies by
$\mathcal A_{\rm IA}^{2}$ instead, so the same redshift and scale trends
appear more strongly and the three panels no longer share one amplitude
ordering.

\subsection{Sampling the 13-parameter family and the data split}

The 13 parameters are four redshift and luminosity controls, five pivot-scale
shape parameters, and four indices that evolve $S$ with redshift.
Table~\ref{tab:sampling-bounds} lists the data-generation bounds.  We draw
100,000 combinations from those ranges.  Each combination produces a surface
on 31 redshift points from $z=0$ to $z=3$ and 101 logarithmically spaced
wavenumbers from $k=0.01$ to $10\,\mathrm{Mpc}^{-1}$.

Random draws can miss failures at the edge of a high-dimensional box, so
before those bounds were fixed we also evaluated 8,192 random samples and all
$2^{13}=8{,}192$ corners.  Every one of those surfaces was finite and
positive.

\reportfigure{sampling_amplitude_ensemble.pdf}{Distribution of 8,192 random
sampling-range validation surfaces at $z=0$ and $z=3$.  In each panel the
horizontal axis is wavenumber $k$ and the vertical axis is
$\mathcal A_\Theta$.  The shaded regions contain the central 98\% and 90\%
of the surfaces at each $k$, the black curve is the median, and the colored
curves are the five surfaces with the largest internal dynamic range.  All
evaluated surfaces are finite and strictly positive.}{fig:sampling-ensemble}

Evaluating a method on the surfaces used to fit it would overstate
generalization, so the stored split contains 80,000 training surfaces,
10,000 validation surfaces, and 10,000 test surfaces.  Training fits the PCA
basis and the autoencoder; validation is used to monitor reconstruction
quality during training; and the test subset remains unread until those
choices are frozen.  Normalization statistics and the PCA basis are computed
from training data only.

The dataset is a controlled compression test: every surface shares the same
$(k,z)$ grid, while the 13 parameters vary.  The next section asks whether
those $3{,}131$ grid values can be represented by a much smaller latent
vector.
