\section{Dimensionality reduction with PCA and autoencoders}
\label{sec:autoencoder}

The response family in Section~\ref{sec:model} provides a controlled test
of how accurately a short representation can preserve variation across
scale and redshift. We compare principal component analysis (PCA) with two
nonlinear methods: a convolutional autoencoder and a PCA-assisted
autoencoder (PCA-AE). Each method reconstructs the same response surface
from $L\in\{2,4,6,8,10\}$ coordinates. Varying $L$ reveals the balance
between representation length and reconstruction accuracy.

\subsection{Input representation and normalization}
\label{sec:input-representation}

For each sampled parameter combination $\bm{\Theta}_i$, we form the
$N_z\times N_k$ log-amplitude surface
\begin{equation}
  Y_i(k,z)=\log_{10}\mathcal A_{\bm{\Theta}_i}(k,z),
  \qquad
  X_i(k,z)=\frac{Y_i(k,z)-\overline Y(k,z)}{\sigma}.
  \label{eq:surface-normalization}
\end{equation}
The logarithm reduces the large amplitude range and makes multiplicative
differences easier to represent. Here $\overline Y$ is the mean training
surface, and $\sigma$ is the root-mean-square deviation from that mean,
averaged over all training surfaces and grid points. Subtracting the mean
focuses learning on variations within the family. Dividing by one global
scale keeps their relative sizes across the grid unchanged; dividing each
grid point by its own standard deviation would change that weighting.

The normalization statistics are fitted using training data and held fixed
for validation and final test evaluation. All methods are evaluated after
undoing the normalization,
$\widehat Y_i=\sigma\widehat X_i+\overline Y$, and, where physical
amplitude errors are required, applying
$\widehat{\mathcal A}_{\bm{\Theta}_i}=10^{\widehat Y_i}$.
The common grid has $N_z=31$ and $N_k=101$ as defined in
Section~\ref{sec:model-sampling}. Writing its shape as $N_z\times N_k$
keeps the distinction between redshift and wavenumber explicit.

\subsection{Linear and nonlinear compression}
\label{sec:compression-principles}

PCA supplies the linear reference. It identifies orthogonal patterns of
variation in the training surfaces and orders them by the variance they
describe. Flattening $X_i$ into
$\bm{x}_i=\operatorname{vec}(X_i)\in\mathbb R^{N_zN_k}$
changes its arrangement without losing information. If $\bm v_a$ is a
unit-length principal component, a rank-$L$ reconstruction is
\begin{equation}
  c_{ia}=\bm v_a^{\mathsf T}\bm x_i,
  \qquad
  \widehat{\bm x}_i^{\,\mathrm{PCA}}
  =\sum_{a=1}^{L}c_{ia}\bm v_a.
  \label{eq:pca-reconstruction}
\end{equation}
The components are fitted only to training data. Because the normalization
uses a single global scale, these are the same component directions as for
the centered log-surfaces. Retaining fewer components discards some
variation, so the accuracy of this approximation must be measured on
held-out surfaces.

An autoencoder instead learns an encoder and decoder,
\begin{equation}
  \bm h_i=f_\phi(X_i)\in\mathbb R^L,
  \qquad
  \widehat X_i=g_\theta(\bm h_i).
  \label{eq:encoder-decoder}
\end{equation}
The parameters $\phi$ and $\theta$ are adjusted to reduce reconstruction
error. Nonlinear transformations allow the same $L$ coordinates to
represent relationships that a fixed linear combination may approximate
less accurately. The latent vector $\bm h_i$ is the bottleneck: no skip
connection carries input information around it. Comparing PCA and
autoencoders at equal $L$ tests the accuracy of equally long
representations. It does not require a latent coordinate to correspond to
one of the $N_\Theta$ generating parameters.

\subsection{Convolutional autoencoder}
\label{sec:conv1d-autoencoder}

The convolutional autoencoder learns directly from $X_i$. It treats the
$N_z$ redshift samples as input channels, each containing a sequence of
$N_k$ wavenumbers. A one-dimensional convolution applies the same local
filter at successive positions along $k$ and combines information across
channels. This design uses the ordered grid to learn patterns spanning
neighbouring scales. After the first layer, channels are learned
combinations of the input; they no longer represent individual redshifts.

We investigate three-, four-, and five-layer convolutional encoders, with
corresponding changes in their dense layers. Figure~\ref{fig:ae-architecture}
shows the three-layer architecture reported in
Section~\ref{sec:results}. Three stride-2 convolutions, with kernel lengths
5, 5, and 3, shorten the wavenumber sequences while increasing the number
of channels. The final $256\times13$ feature array is flattened and
compressed through dense layers of widths 256, 64, and 16 to $L$.
The decoder uses dense layers and transposed convolutions to restore the
original grid dimensions. These operations learn a reconstruction; they
are not exact inverses of the encoder. Hidden layers use the smooth
nonlinear SiLU activation, while the latent and output layers are linear.
The network uses neither dropout nor internal normalization layers.

\subsection{PCA-assisted autoencoder}
\label{sec:pca-ae}

PCA-AE tests whether nonlinear compression remains useful after a linear
projection has already captured most of the surface variation.
It first projects each normalized surface $X_i$ onto a fixed basis of
$R=30$ principal components. Each coefficient is centered and divided by
its training standard deviation before entering the neural network.
This gives coefficients with different natural sizes comparable input
scales. Both the basis and these statistics remain fixed during network
training.

The intermediate rank $R$ and final latent width $L$ have different roles.
Ordinary PCA in the matched comparison keeps $L$ components; PCA-AE
retains $R$ components before learning a nonlinear $R\to L\to R$ map.
A linear basis can require more than $N_\Theta$ components because the
surface depends nonlinearly on its generating parameters.
The three-hidden-layer encoder has widths 256, 64, and 16, and the decoder
uses the reverse sequence, as shown in
Figure~\ref{fig:pca-ae-architecture}. Hidden layers use SiLU, and the
latent and output layers are linear. The reconstructed coefficients are
returned to their original scales and combined with the fixed PCA
patterns to recover $\widehat X_i$. The inverse normalization in
Section~\ref{sec:input-representation} then restores the log-surface
and physical response amplitude.

Rank 30 was chosen to make the initial projection accurate before testing
further compression. Among the ranks 25--30 evaluated on the complete
validation set, rank 28 was the smallest with a maximum physical relative
error below 5\%; retaining 30 components reduced that maximum to 4.50\%.
This threshold is a practical reconstruction criterion, not a precision
requirement derived from cosmological observations.

The neural network can mix the retained coefficients, but reconstructed
variations are restricted to the fixed PCA patterns. Variation in discarded
components therefore supplies a reconstruction floor. At $L=6$, the
three-hidden-layer PCA-AE contains 51,076 trainable parameters, compared
with 2,041,989 for the three-layer convolutional model. PCA-AE also stores
its fixed basis. These methods consequently differ in both representation
and model size; an accuracy difference alone cannot isolate the effect of
convolution.

\subsection{Training and model selection}
\label{sec:training-selection}

Training asks the decoder to reproduce its input surface. For a batch of
$B$ surfaces, the convolutional network minimizes
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal L_{\mathrm{MSE}}
  =\frac{1}{B N_z N_k}
  \sum_{i=1}^{B}\sum_{j=1}^{N_z}\sum_{\ell=1}^{N_k}
  \left(\widehat X_{ij\ell}-X_{ij\ell}\right)^2.
  \label{eq:reconstruction-loss}
\end{equation}
This objective gives each sampled grid point equal weight in normalized
log-amplitude space. PCA-AE uses a weighted coefficient error: the
weights account for coefficient standardization and express the
retained-component error in the same normalized-surface units.
Its full reconstruction error additionally includes the fixed discarded
PCA contribution. Validation monitoring includes both contributions.

AdamW updates the network parameters using batches of training surfaces;
weight decay discourages unnecessarily large weights. A reduction in the
learning rate permits smaller updates when validation improvement slows.
Early stopping ends training after sustained lack of improvement, and
evaluation uses the saved checkpoint selected by validation error rather
than the final training state. Table~\ref{tab:training-config-details}
records the settings and the extended budgets of the reported PCA-AE
runs. The different model sizes and training budgets should be considered
when comparing their performance.

We compare latent widths using validation reconstructions, with 99.9\%
recovered variance as a reference target alongside physical relative-error
diagnostics. This target measures compression of the sampled response
family; it does not establish adequate accuracy for cosmological inference.
The first tested width meeting it need not be the smallest possible width.
Each reported neural configuration has one training seed, so its
performance has no estimate of variation between independent training
runs. The test split is reserved for evaluation after model and training
choices have been fixed.

\subsection{Reconstruction metrics and interpretation}
\label{sec:reconstruction-metrics}

A small average loss can coexist with a poorly reconstructed surface or a
large error in a narrow part of the grid. We therefore measure overall
accuracy, physical amplitude errors, and the distribution of errors across
surfaces. The definitions in Table~\ref{tab:validation-metrics} apply to
any data subset. Section~\ref{sec:results} uses the same 10,000 validation
surfaces for the reported comparison.

Recovered variance measures improvement over predicting the training mean
surface for every input:
\begin{equation}
  V_{\mathrm{rec}}
  =1-
  \frac{\displaystyle\sum_{i,j,\ell}
    \left(\widehat Y_{ij\ell}-Y_{ij\ell}\right)^2}
  {\displaystyle\sum_{i,j,\ell}
    \left(Y_{ij\ell}-\overline Y_{j\ell}\right)^2}.
  \label{eq:recovered-variance}
\end{equation}
The sums cover the evaluated surfaces and all $N_zN_k$ grid points.
A perfect reconstruction gives $V_{\mathrm{rec}}=1$; predicting the mean
gives zero, and worse predictions can give negative values.
Log RMSE measures the residual size in the same log-amplitude space.
Both metrics use the same squared residuals, providing two descriptions
of the global reconstruction error rather than independent accuracy tests.
Neither quantity directly describes the fractional error at a particular
scale and redshift.

To assess that error, we return to the physical amplitude. Define
\begin{equation}
  e_{ij\ell}
  =\left|10^{\widehat Y_{ij\ell}-Y_{ij\ell}}-1\right|,
  \qquad
  r_i=\sqrt{\frac{1}{N_zN_k}
    \sum_{j=1}^{N_z}\sum_{\ell=1}^{N_k}e_{ij\ell}^{\,2}}.
  \label{eq:physical-relative-errors}
\end{equation}
Here $e_{ij\ell}$ is the absolute fractional error at one grid point,
and $r_i$ is the relative RMSE of one complete surface. Percentiles of
$r_i$ describe how accuracy varies across the sampled family: at the 95th
percentile, approximately 95\% of evaluated surfaces have a smaller
relative RMSE. This is distinct from a percentile of individual grid-point
errors or of each surface's maximum error.

Together, these diagnostics distinguish good average reconstruction from
reliable performance across the family. They weight the sampled grid
directly. Establishing the accuracy needed for a lensing analysis will
also require propagating reconstruction errors into its observables and
cosmological parameter estimates.
