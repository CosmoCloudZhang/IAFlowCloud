\section{Compressing the surfaces with an autoencoder}
\label{sec:autoencoder}

Section~\ref{sec:model} constructed the surface dataset.  Here we transform and
normalize each surface before passing it through three one-dimensional
convolutional layers and a two-number bottleneck.  The decoder reconstructs the
full grid from those two values.  Section~\ref{sec:results} compares its
validation accuracy with PCA.

\subsection{Input transformation and normalization}

Before the encoder sees a surface we write

\begin{equation}
  Y(k,z)=\log_{10}\mathcal A_\Theta(k,z),
\end{equation}

because the positive amplitudes span a wide range.  The network input is then
the centered and scaled log-surface

\begin{equation}
  \bm{x}=\frac{Y-\overline{Y}}{\sigma},
\end{equation}

where $\overline{Y}$ is the mean training surface and $\sigma$ is the global
root-mean-square deviation from that surface.  The logarithm is applied
pointwise.  Both $\overline{Y}$ and $\sigma$ are computed from the training
split and then applied unchanged to validation and test data.  Using one global
scale, rather than a different scale at each $(k,z)$, keeps the relative size
of features across the grid and avoids leakage from the held-out subsets.

\subsection{From 3,131 values to a two-number bottleneck}

An autoencoder contains an \emph{encoder} and a \emph{decoder}.  The encoder
turns a surface $\bm{x}$ into a short latent vector $\bm{z}$,

\begin{equation}
  \bm{z}=f_{\phi}(\bm{x}),
  \qquad
  \widehat{\bm{x}}=g_{\theta}(\bm{z}),
\end{equation}

and the decoder tries to reconstruct the input.  We set
$\bm{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}$.  Because no skip connection bypasses this
bottleneck, every reconstructed surface must pass through two numbers.

One-dimensional convolutions first collect nearby structure along wavenumber.
Dense layers then summarize the whole surface.  The bottleneck keeps only two
coordinates, from which the decoder must rebuild the full grid.

\subsection{Why use one-dimensional convolutions?}

The input is arranged as 31 redshift channels, each containing a 101-point
sequence in wavenumber.  A one-dimensional convolution slides along the
wavenumber direction and learns local scale-dependent patterns.  At each position,
a small kernel looks at neighboring wavenumber values and combines them using
learned weights.  As the kernel moves along the $k$ axis, the same set of weights
can detect similar local patterns at different scales.  Different
redshift channels are mixed by the learned filters.  This design respects more
of the surface structure than treating all 3,131 values as an unrelated list.

With a two-number bottleneck, the network has 2,039,969 trainable parameters.
It uses SiLU activations, no dropout, no normalization layers inside the
network, and no skip connections.  A skip connection would allow information
to bypass the bottleneck and would invalidate the comparison with
two-component PCA.

\subsection{Training objective and model selection}

The network minimizes the mean squared reconstruction error in normalized
log-amplitude space,

\begin{equation}
  \mathcal L_{\rm MSE}
  =\frac{1}{N N_z N_k}
  \sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N_z}\sum_{\ell=1}^{N_k}
  \left(\widehat{x}_{ij\ell}-x_{ij\ell}\right)^2.
\end{equation}

Here $N$ is the batch size, while $N_z=31$ and $N_k=101$.  The current training
configuration uses AdamW with learning rate $2\times10^{-4}$, weight decay
$10^{-5}$, and gradient clipping at unit norm.  Surfaces are processed in
batches of 512.  A ReduceLROnPlateau scheduler multiplies the learning rate by
$1/2$ and has a validation-loss patience of 12 epochs.  The schedule permits
at most 1,000 epochs and stops early after 50 epochs without sufficient
validation improvement.  Checkpoint selection is also based on validation
loss.  The configured seed is 42.  These settings define the current shared
training template; the preliminary autoencoder metrics in
Section~\ref{sec:results} come from a separate 25-epoch run, not from a
completed run of this longer schedule.

\subsection{What must be checked beyond the average loss?}

An average MSE can hide a small region or a difficult surface with a much larger
error.  We therefore evaluate error in log space, relative error after
converting back to $\mathcal A_\Theta$, recovered variance, and the distribution
of per-surface errors.  Physical relative error is
$\lvert 10^{(\widehat Y-Y)}-1\rvert$.  Section~\ref{sec:results} reports the
available validation summaries, including the 95th percentile of per-surface
relative RMSE.  These tail errors are diagnostics; they are not assigned an
invented pass threshold.
